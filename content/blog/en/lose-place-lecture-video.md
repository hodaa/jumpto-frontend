The usual advice for losing focus while studying is about attention: put the phone away, use a timer, sit somewhere quiet. That advice is mostly correct and it does not address the actual mechanical problem, which is this:

**You did not lose focus. You lost your place.**

Something interrupted you — a message, a door, a question. You come back, the video has kept playing, and you are now watching material you already understood while the part you needed continues unseen. So you rewind. Then you overshoot. Then you forward to check, and now you have lost it again.

This is not a discipline failure. It is a navigation failure, and it has a concrete fix.

## Why rewinding does not work

Replaying is a blunt instrument. It is expensive — you pay 47 minutes of runtime to recover 40 seconds of content. And it is imprecise, because you are estimating the position from a vague memory of what was said.

There is a better way to think about recovery. A precise anchor beats a rewind. If you can name the word or sentence the professor was on when you left, the moment is findable in seconds, and you resume *mid-thought* instead of restarting a section.

## Getting an anchor before you stop

This is the habit that makes the rest work. Before you pause, write down or remember **one distinctive phrase** from the last thing you understood. Not a concept, not a topic — a literal string of words. "The unfunded portion of the liability" is a good anchor. "The second part" is not.

Distinctive beats correct. The goal is not an accurate quote, it is a *rare* string of words that the transcript contains in exactly one place.

## Resuming without rewatching

1. Search the transcript for the phrase you anchored.
2. Confirm you are in the right place by reading the sentence around the match.
3. Jump to that timestamp and continue.

The difference is that you resume in a few seconds, in the middle of the thought, with the material still coherent. Compare that to rewinding five minutes, fast-forwarding, and trying to work out where you were.

## The honesty problem with transcripts

Reading a transcript instead of watching sounds efficient, and it genuinely is for the parts you understand. It is a poor substitute for the parts that require seeing something demonstrated, written, or drawn. A lecturer's worked example on a whiteboard is not recoverable from a text transcript.

The useful split is: **transcript and search to navigate, video to actually learn.** Using search to find your place is not a shortcut around watching the lecture. It is what makes watching the lecture bearable.

## The multi-hour case

If you are working through a long recording, search is not optional. A three-hour lecture is not a thing anyone holds in working memory, and the parts you skip are the parts you will not remember. Being able to search, drop in, and drop out is the difference between attending something and sampling it.
